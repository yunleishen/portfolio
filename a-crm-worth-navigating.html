02 — Sugar CRM · 2024–25

A CRM Worth Navigating

(5 min read) · (Shipped)

Sugar CRM is where every VBG employee spends the working day. Years of growth without a designer had buried the work four to six clicks deep, and claim submissions had quietly fallen by half. I rebuilt the information architecture — and let five teams decide what it should be.

Sugar CRM

(My role)

(Timeline)

(Scope)

IA & navigation · companion project

(Context)

The tool five teams live in

Sugar holds everything: leads, veterans, claims, documents, disabilities. It grew field by field, shaped by whoever needed something that week, and no one had ever stepped back to ask what shape it should have. By the time I took it on, the structure itself was the cost — in productivity, in morale, and eventually in submissions.

The hard part was never the diagnosis. It was that five teams had built eight-hour-a-day habits inside this structure, and any fix worth doing would break all of them at once.

(Owned)

IA redesign · Research & card sorting · New sitemap · Page-layout logic · Rollout plan

(Partnered on)

Engineering build · Role permissions — companion case

(Constraint)

SugarCRM let us restructure navigation, pages, and fields — not the record model.

(Problem · Diagnosis)

What was actually broken

Interviews and field studies with each role surfaced three structural problems:

1

One navigation for everyone. Crowded with links most roles never used; no secondary navigation; the same features in different places on different pages.

2

Everything was deep. Key information sat 3–5 clicks away; related content was scattered; critical actions like upload had no consistent home.

3

Key details hidden in plain sight. Pages omitted basics like file names or the associated veteran; sorting followed no logic users recognized.

(Strategy)

Patch, restructure, or replace

A new IA from heuristics would have taken a week, the problems were that visible. But five teams would have to relearn the tool they use eight hours a day, and a designer’s opinion would not survive that fight. Evidence would. First, the path:

A (Ruled out)

Patch the worst pages

Low risk, no retraining — and no ceiling raised. The problems were architectural; this is polishing rooms in a house with a broken floor plan.

Fixes symptoms, not the plan.

B (Chosen)

Restructure the IA inside SugarCRM

The platform let us rebuild navigation, pages, and fields, but not the record model. Months of work, five teams retrained.

Months of work — and the ceiling comes down.

C (Ruled out)

Replace the CRM entirely

Floated by ops leadership. But every documented problem was structural, not platform-specific — and a mid-year data migration the business could not afford.

New tool, same rot.

(Process · Redesign)

Rebuilding the map before the rooms

(Waypoint 01 · Chart the terrain)

Let users draw the architecture, card sorting across roles

Participants from every role sorted each page and feature around their real workflows. The new IA came straight from their mental models and put every key page within two clicks.

(Waypoint 02 · Redraw the main roads)

Rebuilding the top level

Navigation keeps only the most used links; the homepage now leads with work progress, key updates, and each role's top pages. The day starts there, not three menus deep.

(Waypoint 03 · Fit each room to its use)

Match the layout to the data, not the template

One rule settled every page debate: pages people act on get cards with key info on the face; pages people scan keep tables, plus the identity columns every sort demanded. Deciding the rule first meant no layout ever got relitigated.

(Reflection)

When several roles share one system, the IA is the product. The deeper lesson was authority: I could not argue five teams into a new structure, but I could hand them the cards and let them argue each other into it. The card sort was not just research — it was the political strategy.
