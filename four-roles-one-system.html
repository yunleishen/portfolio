Four Roles, One System

In Sugar CRM, everyone could edit everything. Work was being silently undone, data conflicted, and nobody owned anything. After rebuilding the architecture, I redesigned how each role moves through a claim, so five teams could finally share one system without colliding.

PROBLEM

No permissions, no ownership, no order

Every role had access to everything, no permission controls, anyone could edit any record. Duplicate entries and conflicting data were routine, and people unknowingly undid each other's work. Claim submissions dropped; revenue followed.

Interviews with team leaders mapped the workflow and scoped it: two roles worked in a third-party platform and didn't belong in Sugar. The four that did:

Solution

Restrict, sequence, and sign the work

Solution 01

Show each role only its own work

Dashboards are separated by role, logging in lands you on your tasks and nothing else. Visibility and the progress bar follow role and task stage, and each phase ends with a review checkpoint that stops duplicates and unintended changes from leaking downstream.

Solution 02

Two kinds of work, two kinds of flow

Intake is sequential, so it got a linear step by step flow; review work is non-linear, so reviewers got priority-ordered tabs with free movement back. Same system, two rhythms.

Solution 03

Status defines the action

Every status maps to one tone and one action: six tones tell you at a glance whether something is yours, waiting, or urgent, and what to do next. Checkboxes beside each document let reviewers mark progress and resume cleanly after interruptions.

Solution 04

Make accountability an interaction, not a policy

Submitting takes a deliberate “Mark as Complete”,  a confirmation that puts a name on the work, and an immutable System Information record traces who created, accepted, and rejected each item. Careless errors dropped measurably.

Reflection

The fixes that mattered most were small: organizing tasks in the order people do them, and asking for one deliberate confirmation. Turning a messy free-for-all into a smooth, owned process was the win that counted.
