01 — VBG Portal · 2025–26

The End of the Paper Trail

(14 min read) · (Shipped)

Veteran Benefits Guide runs disability claims for veterans, and ran them entirely on paper. Every claim moved at the speed of a staff member carrying it. I led the design of the platform that took the whole thing online, and cut processing time nearly in half.

(Product)

VBG Portal

(My role)

Product Designer

(Timeline)

2025 – 2026

(Scope)

End-to-end claim filing, 0→1

(Context)

The manual process was the ceiling on the business

Before I joined, every step of a veteran’s claim was done by hand, and the claim reached the VA on day 62 without the veteran ever seeing its status. Serving more veterans meant hiring more people.

(Research)

Two months in the field, one root cause

As the first designer I had no research to inherit, so I spent two months shadowing every role and logging every question and blocker.

The notes clustered into four frictions, all tracing to one root cause: responsibilities, forms, appointments and status lived in people, not the system.

That set the direction: not a nicer internal tool for employees, but moving the process into the system so veterans can complete their own part directly.

(Principles)

Three design principles

(Problem 1 · Process)

18 touchpoints became 7 steps with clear boundaries

Problem
Kickbacks cost days. The boundaries were wrong.
My read
Who is each step for: the veteran or the paper?
Action
Manual pass → AI mapping → ops.
Result
18 steps → 7, one owner each.

The veteran appears in only three of 18 touchpoints; the rest are employee handoffs with repeated kickbacks.

(Three passes)

From 18 to 7

I cut in three passes: deleted steps that existed only for the paper, used AI to map dependencies and merge parallel work, then went line by line with ops. Ops insisted on keeping the underwriter’s and case review specialist’s independent reviews, and I agreed, because they protect the veteran.

One rule removed the kickbacks, and it lives in the system, not in the training manual.

(Problem 2 · DBQ)

An unreadable DBQ became a form veterans finish alone

Problem
Opaque language, fields left blank.
My read
A language and interaction problem.
Action
Rewrite the language → test the shape → answer in the form.
Result
Case managers: from required to fallback.

The DBQ is the VA’s medical questionnaire, a dozen-plus pages of clinical terms that veterans can’t finish. The form itself had to change, in its language, its shape, and by building case managers’ experience into the interface.

One page of fourteen in the ankle form.

(Step 01 · Language)

A tug of war over language

My line was that the veteran has to understand it; the medical consultant’s was that nothing can drift from VA regulations, HIPAA or clinical intent. Neither could give, so we built a pipeline where AI produces volume and humans make the calls.

Three boundaries nothing could break; the example below is illustrative.

(Step 02 · Structure)

Content and information architecture first

I grouped every question and marked required, conditional and dependent fields, which fixed the form’s shape before navigation or cards.

(Step 03 · Navigation)

Let testing decide the navigation

I leaned toward one question at a time, but testing with 8 veterans showed a scrolling column won on every measure. What they wanted was control.

(Step 04 · Guidance)

Anticipating the questions

I logged where case managers stepped in most and how they helped, and turned it into three card types: narrative (example answer plus checklist), severity scale (real-life scenarios, not 1–4) and measurement (an animated demo).

The three cards went into the design system I built from scratch, so each new DBQ is configuration rather than redesign.

(Problem 3 · Appointment)

Appointments that hold: guardrail and experience in one design

Problem
Veterans reschedule around VBG.
My read
Three parties’ interests pull apart.
Action
Status chain + delayed disclosure.
Result
Disclosure, reminder and deadline all at T-2.

After booking, VBG handed veterans the network’s contact details, so they rescheduled directly and VBG lost track. The veteran, the network and VBG each wanted something different, and a better experience alone couldn’t solve it.

(Action 01 · Status)

Govern the process with status

Each step of the status chain takes a manual confirmation, and cancel and reschedule only happen in the system, more than 2 days before the appointment.

(Action 02 · Disclosure)

Delayed disclosure, tested

The network’s address and phone stay hidden until the appointment is close, so rescheduling comes back through the system. That only works with a clear promise of when details arrive and timing that matches when veterans need them, which I tested.

I tested two reveal points before settling on one.

So it landed on two days out, where disclosure, reminder and deadline coincide: guardrail and experience hold in one design.

(Problem 4 · Status)

Invisible progress: the right granularity of transparency

Problem
Tracker shipped, progress calls kept coming.
My read
Wrong granularity, not missing transparency.
Action
Rebuild around what veterans ask.
Result
Expected time + sub-steps.

The tracker showed all seven steps from day one, yet veterans kept calling. Their questions clustered into three: how many more days, where is it now, and does it need me.

The first two rounds missed in opposite directions.

The fix wasn’t a point between the two; it was changing the question from “what happened inside the claim?” to “which three questions will the veteran ask?” Anything that answered none of them was removed.

(Impact)

The numbers, and the structural change behind them

(Reflection)

What I took away

The best design is never the first version. Working across many stakeholders taught me that I am not always right: I used to assume that standing with the veteran was enough, but rewriting the DBQ meant honoring regulations I couldn’t bend.

The real work was serving veterans better within those rules, and that tension, pulled hard in both directions, often sparked my best ideas.
